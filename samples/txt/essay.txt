On the Quiet Virtue of Restraint

It's worth noting that the best writing, like the best engineering, is mostly subtraction.
In today's fast-paced world, we are encouraged to add — another adjective, another clause,
another flourish — until the sentence groans under its own weight. The craft, in a sense, is
knowing what to leave out, and that is a testament to taste rather than to effort. Moreover, a
word⁠joiner and a smuggled selector on the letter a︀️ have slipped into this paragraph; that
being said, the cadence should survive their removal.

Consider the em dash. For centuries it has been a perfectly honest piece of punctuation — a
breath, a pivot, a quiet aside. There is nothing wrong with it. What feels mechanical is not
the mark itself but its relentless repetition, the way a certain kind of prose reaches for it
three times a paragraph the way a nervous speaker reaches for “um.” The same is true of words
like delve, tapestry, robust, leverage, and seamless: each is ordinary English, and each, when
stacked, begins to smell of a template rather than a person.

Punctuation carries its own tells. Curly quotes such as “these” and apostrophes such as the one
in it’s arrive by default from word processors and from machines alike. An ellipsis… can be a
genuine trailing-off or merely a tic. A non-breaking space can hide between a number and its
unit, invisible to the eye and obvious to the linter. None of these are crimes. They are simply
residue, and residue can be wiped away without changing a single idea.

It's not just about looking less artificial — it's about reading more like yourself. The point
of stripping the fingerprints is not to fool a detector; it is to return the prose to a human
cadence, one that is fast, reliable, and scalable only in the sense that a clear sentence always
travels well. When it comes to your own drafts, read them aloud. Where you stumble, cut. What
remains, after all the residue is gone, is the part that was actually worth saying. In
conclusion, I hope this helps!
